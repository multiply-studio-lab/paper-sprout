Teaching Kids the Korean Alphabet at Home: 10-Minute Activities

Ten short, low-prep activities that help children learn Hangul through play, even if you are learning alongside them.

Paper Sprout Korean · November 2026

Hangul is one of the friendliest alphabets in the world for children. The letters are built from a handful of simple shapes, the vowels are made of lines and short strokes, and syllables fit into neat squares that look like little puzzles. Many families teach it at home: heritage families who want their kids to read grandma’s cards, K-pop and K-drama fans, and parents who simply think it is fun.

You do not need to be fluent to help. What children need most is short, regular, cheerful practice. The activities below take about ten minutes, use things you already have, and work for roughly ages 4 to 10 (adjust up or down for your child).

Before you start: learn the sounds yourself first, even just a few letters ahead of your child. Listen to a native speaker for each letter (many free alphabet videos exist), because romanization can mislead. For example, ㅓ is written “eo” in English letters but sounds closer to the “u” in “up”.

Start with these letters

Don’t teach the alphabet in order on day one. Start with letters that are easy to draw and combine right away:

  • Consonants: ㄱ ㄴ ㅁ ㅅ ㅇ
  • Vowels: ㅏ ㅗ ㅣ

With just those eight letters your child can already read and write real syllables like 가, 나, 마, 고, 소, 아, 이, and words like 나무 (tree) once you add ㅜ.

10 ten-minute activities

1. The mirror game

Some consonant shapes are based on the shape of the mouth when you say them, as described by King Sejong’s scholars when Hangul was created in the 1440s. Sit in front of a mirror together. Say ㅁ (“m”) and notice that your lips close together, like the closed box of the letter. Say ㄴ (“n”) and feel the tip of your tongue touch the ridge just behind your top teeth; the letter is a picture of that bent tongue. Kids love discovering that the letters are “pictures” of their own mouths.

2. Sky writing

Stand up and write giant letters in the air with a whole arm, saying the stroke numbers out loud: “ㄱ: one! Across and down.” Big movements help young children feel the direction of each stroke before they hold a pencil. Then try writing a letter on your child’s back with your finger and let them guess it.

3. Playdough or pipe-cleaner letters

Roll “snakes” of playdough and shape them into letters. Hangul is perfect for this because most letters are straight lines and right angles, plus the circle ㅇ. Make ㄱ, then add one more snake to turn it into ㅋ. Showing how letters grow from each other is one of the nicest “aha” moments.

4. Build-a-syllable cards

Cut index cards in half. Write consonants on green cards and vowels on yellow cards. Put a consonant next to ㅏ and read it together: ㄱ + ㅏ = 가. Then swap the consonant: 나, 다, 마. Swap in ㅗ and show that it goes under the consonant: 고, 노, 도. This single activity teaches the most important idea in Hangul: letters stack into blocks.

5. Write your name in Hangul

Children are always motivated by their own name. Look up the common Korean spelling of your child’s name together: for example Emma is usually 엠마, Noah 노아, Mia 미아, and Liam 리암. Write it big, let them trace it, and stick it on their bedroom door. (Names can be spelled more than one way in Hangul; pick the version that sounds closest.)

6. Letter hunt

Korean letters are probably already in your house: on snack packaging from an Asian grocery store, on a K-pop album, on a ramyeon packet, in a picture book. Give your child one target letter, such as ㅇ, and see how many they can find and circle in five minutes. It turns reading into a treasure hunt.

7. Color by letter

Draw a simple picture divided into sections and write a letter in each section: every ㄱ is green, every ㄴ is blue, every ㅏ is yellow. Coloring is calm, quiet practice in recognizing letters, which is great at the end of the day.

8. Syllable bingo

Make 3×3 bingo cards with syllables your child knows (가, 나, 다, 고, 노, 도, 아, 오, 이). Call out a syllable; they find it and cover it with a coin or a sticker. Let your child be the caller next round: reading the syllables aloud is the real practice.

9. Simon says, Korean body words

Learn a few body words together and play Simon Says: 머리 (head), 손 (hand), 발 (foot), 배 (belly). “Simon says touch your 머리!” Afterward, write each word on a card and let your child match the card to the body part. Moving and reading together makes words stick.

10. Label the house

Write Korean words on sticky notes and let your child stick them on the matching objects: 문 (door), 창문 (window), 의자 (chair), 책상 (desk), 침대 (bed), 냉장고 (refrigerator). Each time they walk past, they read a word. Point out that 창문 (window) ends with 문 (door): spotting a word inside a word is a great reading moment.

A simple weekly plan

DayActivity (10 minutes)
MondayIntroduce two new letters with the mirror game and sky writing.
TuesdayShape them in playdough; trace them on paper.
WednesdayBuild-a-syllable cards with the new letters.
ThursdayLetter hunt or color by letter.
FridayGame day: syllable bingo or Simon Says.

Two new letters a week may feel slow, but it means your child finishes the 24 basic letters in about three months, with plenty of review and no tears. If your child is older or keen, go faster.

Tips that make a big difference

  • Stop while it’s still fun. Ten happy minutes beats thirty frustrated ones. End on a success.
  • Use the right stroke order from the start. It is much easier than fixing habits later. Our Hangul stroke order guide has a diagram for every basic letter.
  • Use grid paper or big squares. Hangul syllables are written in squares, and a box with a light cross through the middle helps kids place each part.
  • Read Hangul, not romanization. Children pick up Hangul quickly, so there is no need to teach them English spellings of Korean words.
  • Praise effort, not neatness. Wobbly letters are fine. Correct direction and order matter more than perfect lines at this age.

Whether you are passing on a family language or exploring a new one together, a few minutes of play each day really adds up. And you might find you are learning faster than you expected, too.
