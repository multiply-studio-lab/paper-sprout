50 Essential Korean Words for Absolute Beginners

People, questions, everyday verbs, food, time and places: fifty words that show up in almost every beginner conversation, each with a real example sentence.

Paper Sprout Korean · November 2026

When you start Korean, it is tempting to learn long lists of nouns: colors, animals, fruits. They are fun, but they do not help you say much. This list is different. These fifty words were chosen because they let you build sentences right away: talking about yourself, asking questions, saying what you do, and reacting to things.

Every word comes with an example sentence in polite everyday Korean (the style that ends in -요). This is the safest style for a beginner: polite enough for strangers and shop staff, but not stiff.

About the romanization. The small Latin letters under each word follow the Revised Romanization of Korean, which follows pronunciation in many cases (though not all: 학교 is hakgyo even though it sounds like hakkyo). That is why 맛있다 is written masitda rather than matitda, and 좋다 is jota. Use romanization as a crutch for the first week, then try to read the Hangul directly. If you have not learned Hangul yet, start with our Hangul stroke order guide.

Three things to know before the list

Korean drops “I” and “you” a lot. 밥을 먹었어요 can mean “I ate”, “you ate”, or “she ate” depending on context. You will see this in many of the examples below, where the English adds a pronoun that the Korean leaves out.

Little particles mark the role of each word. In 친구를 만나요 (“I meet a friend”), 를 marks the object. In 학교에 가요 (“I go to school”), 에 marks the destination. Do not try to master particles yet; just notice them in the examples.

The verb comes last. Korean word order is usually subject, object, verb: 저는 커피를 마셔요 is literally “I coffee drink”.

Words that are easy to mix up

  • 있다 / 없다: “to have / there is” and “to not have / there isn’t”. 시간이 있어요? “Do you have time?” is one of the most useful sentences you can learn.
  • 알다 / 모르다: Korean has a separate verb for “not know”, so you say 몰라요 rather than 안 알아요.
  • 고맙다 / 반갑다: 고마워요 is “thank you”; 반가워요 is “nice to meet you” or “good to see you”.
  • 형 / 오빠 / 누나 / 언니: “older brother” and “older sister” depend on who is speaking. A man says 형 (older brother) and 누나 (older sister); a woman says 오빠 and 언니. Younger siblings are simply 동생, whoever is speaking.
  • 무엇 and 뭐: 뭐 is the short, spoken form of 무엇. In speech you will hear 뭐예요? (“What is it?”) far more often.

How to study this list

Fifty words is a perfect first goal: small enough to finish in two weeks, big enough to make sentences. Here is a simple plan:

  1. Five words a day. Read the word aloud, write it three times by hand, then copy the example sentence once.
  2. Cover and recall. The next day, cover the Korean column and try to write each word from the English. Mark the ones you missed.
  3. Make one sentence of your own. Swap a single word in an example: 주말에 친구를 만나요 becomes 내일 친구를 만나요. Changing one piece at a time is how sentence patterns stick.
  4. Review on a schedule. Come back to each group after a day, after three days, and after a week. Our guide on learning Korean vocabulary that sticks explains why spacing out review works better than cramming.

By the end of two weeks you will be able to introduce yourself, ask where the restroom is, ask for a glass of water, say what you like, and ask how much something costs, all in polite Korean. That is a real foundation, and every word after this one gets easier.
