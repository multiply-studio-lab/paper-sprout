How to Write Hangul: Stroke Order for All 24 Basic Letters

Step-by-step stroke diagrams for the 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels, plus the two rules that explain almost all of them.

Paper Sprout Korean · November 2026

You can learn to read Hangul in an afternoon, but your handwriting will look shaky for weeks unless you learn one more thing: stroke order. Korean children learn a fixed order for every letter, and that order is the reason their handwriting flows. The good news is that stroke order in Hangul is far simpler than in Chinese or Japanese. There are only 24 basic letters, most have one to three strokes, and nearly every one follows the same two rules.

The two rules

1. Top to bottom. When a letter has parts stacked vertically, write the top part first. That is why the little stroke on top of ㅊ and ㅎ comes first, and why the short vertical of ㅗ comes before its long bar.

2. Left to right. When parts sit side by side, write the left part first. Horizontal strokes are drawn left to right, and vertical strokes are drawn top to bottom.

Whenever you are unsure, ask: “What is on top? What is on the left?” and start there.

How to read the diagrams. Each small box shows one step. The black stroke is the one you write now; gray strokes are already written. The numbered circle marks where the stroke starts, and the arrow shows where it ends.

The 14 basic consonants

Each consonant has a name made of two syllables that shows the letter at the start and at the end of a syllable, like 기역 for ㄱ. The romanization below follows the Revised Romanization of Korean, the official system used on road signs in South Korea. Where two letters are given (g / k), the first is the usual sound at the start of a word and the second is the sound at the end of a syllable.

ㄱ1 stroke
기역 giyeok · sound: g / k
One stroke: across, then straight down. Next to a vertical vowel (가) the down-stroke slants to the lower left; above a horizontal vowel (고) it drops straight down.
ㄴ1 stroke
니은 nieun · sound: n
One stroke: down, then across to the right. It mirrors ㄱ.
ㄷ2 strokes
디귿 digeut · sound: d / t
Top bar first, then an ㄴ-shape underneath.
ㄹ3 strokes
리을 rieul · sound: r / l
Three strokes: a ㄱ-shape, the middle bar, then an ㄴ-shape. Think “ㄱ + ㅡ + ㄴ”.
ㅁ3 strokes
미음 mieum · sound: m
Left side first, then top and right side in one stroke, then the bottom bar closes the box.
ㅂ4 strokes
비읍 bieup · sound: b / p
Two verticals (left, then right), then the middle bar, then the bottom bar.
ㅅ2 strokes
시옷 siot · sound: s / t
Left-falling stroke first, then the right leg starts partway down the first stroke.
ㅇ1 stroke
이응 ieung · sound: silent / ng
One stroke, starting at the top and going counterclockwise. Silent at the start of a syllable, “ng” at the end.
ㅈ2 strokes
지읒 jieut · sound: j / t
Two strokes in handwriting: a ㄱ-like top that sweeps down-left, then the right leg. Some fonts print it as a bar over ㅅ; both read the same.
ㅊ3 strokes
치읓 chieut · sound: ch / t
A short top stroke, then write ㅈ underneath. The top tick may be vertical or slanted depending on the typeface.
ㅋ2 strokes
키읔 kieuk · sound: k (aspirated) / k
Write ㄱ, then add a short middle bar.
ㅌ3 strokes
티읕 tieut · sound: t (aspirated) / t
Top bar, middle bar, then an ㄴ-shape. Korean school charts teach this order.
ㅍ4 strokes
피읖 pieup · sound: p (aspirated) / p
Top bar, left vertical, right vertical, bottom bar.
ㅎ3 strokes
히읗 hieut · sound: h / t
Short top tick, the long bar, then ㅇ underneath (counterclockwise from the top). The top tick may be short and flat or short and vertical, depending on the typeface.

Families make consonants easier

Many consonants are built by adding strokes to a simpler one, and the stroke order follows the same logic:

  • ㄱ → ㅋ: write ㄱ, then add the middle bar.
  • ㄴ → ㄷ → ㅌ: ㄷ is a top bar plus ㄴ; ㅌ adds a middle bar before the ㄴ-shape.
  • ㅅ → ㅈ → ㅊ: each adds a stroke on top.
  • ㅇ → ㅎ: two strokes on top, then the circle.

The five double consonants (ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ) are simply the single letter written twice, left one first, each a little narrower.

The 10 basic vowels

Every vowel is built from a long line plus one or two short strokes. Vertical vowels (ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅣ) sit to the right of the consonant, as in 가. Horizontal vowels (ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ) sit underneath it, as in 고. When a vowel starts a syllable, it is written with a silent ㅇ in front: 아, 오, 이.

ㅏ2 strokes
아 a · sound: a (as in “father”)
Long vertical first, then the short stroke to the right.
ㅑ3 strokes
야 ya · sound: ya
Vertical first, then two short strokes to the right, top one first.
ㅓ2 strokes
어 eo · sound: eo (close to the “u” in “up”)
Short stroke first (left to right), then the long vertical.
ㅕ3 strokes
여 yeo · sound: yeo
Two short strokes, top first, then the long vertical.
ㅗ2 strokes
오 o · sound: o (a pure “o”, lips rounded)
Short vertical on top first, then the long horizontal under it.
ㅛ3 strokes
요 yo · sound: yo
Two short verticals (left, then right), then the long horizontal.
ㅜ2 strokes
우 u · sound: u (as in “moon”)
Long horizontal first, then the short vertical hanging below.
ㅠ3 strokes
유 yu · sound: yu
Long horizontal first, then two short verticals, left then right.
ㅡ1 stroke
으 eu · sound: eu (say “oo” with your lips spread, not rounded)
One horizontal stroke, left to right.
ㅣ1 stroke
이 i · sound: i (as in “ski”)
One vertical stroke, top to bottom.

Notice the pattern: for ㅏ the long line comes first because it is on the left; for ㅓ the short stroke comes first because it is on the left. For ㅗ the short stroke is on top, so it goes first; for ㅜ the long line is on top. You do not need to memorize ten separate orders, only the two rules.

Putting letters into syllable blocks

Korean is never written one letter at a time in a row. Letters are grouped into square syllable blocks, and the same two rules decide the order inside each block:

Block shapeExampleWriting order
Consonant + vertical vowel가ㄱ then ㅏ (left to right)
Consonant + horizontal vowel고ㄱ then ㅗ (top to bottom)
With a final consonant각ㄱ, ㅏ, then the final ㄱ at the bottom
Horizontal vowel + final곰ㄱ, ㅗ, then ㅁ

Letters change shape a little depending on where they sit. In 가, the ㄱ is tall and its down-stroke slants toward the lower left. In 고, the ㄱ is short and wide. A final consonant at the bottom (the batchim) is written flatter so the whole block stays square. Practice on paper with a square grid: draw a cross through the middle of each square and aim to fill it evenly.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Drawing ㅇ clockwise. Start at the top and go counterclockwise. It feels odd at first if you are used to drawing circles clockwise, but it makes ㅎ and fast handwriting much smoother.
  • Writing ㅁ as four separate lines. It is three strokes: left side, then the top and right side in one motion, then the bottom.
  • Splitting ㄱ or ㄴ into two strokes. Each is a single stroke with a corner.
  • Making ㅓ and ㅏ too similar. The short stroke should be clearly on one side of the long line, at about its middle height.
  • Confusing ㄹ with the number 2 or a “Z”. It has three distinct strokes and three flat horizontal lines.

A 15-minute practice routine

  1. Look (2 minutes). Pick four letters. Say each name out loud and trace the steps above with your finger.
  2. Trace (5 minutes). Trace each letter over a light gray model five times, saying the stroke numbers as you go.
  3. Write (5 minutes). Write each letter in empty grid squares without looking at the model, then compare.
  4. Combine (3 minutes). Add ㅏ and ㅗ to each consonant and write the syllables: 가 고 나 노 다 도.

Four letters a day gets you through all 24 basic letters in under a week. After that, move on to the double consonants and the combined vowels (ㅐ ㅔ ㅘ ㅝ and friends), which reuse every stroke you already know.
