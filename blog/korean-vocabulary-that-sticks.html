How to Learn Korean Vocabulary That Sticks

Handwriting, spaced review and example sentences: what each one actually does for you, and a simple weekly routine that combines them.

Paper Sprout Korean · November 2026

Almost every Korean learner hits the same wall. You learn twenty new words on Monday, feel great, and by Friday you can remember six. The problem is rarely effort. It is usually method: reading a list over and over feels productive, but it mostly builds familiarity, the feeling of “I’ve seen this before”, not the ability to pull the word out of your head when you need it.

This guide covers four habits that genuinely help. None of them is a secret or a hack, and we will be upfront about what each one can and cannot do.

1. Learn words inside example sentences

A word on its own is a label. A word in a sentence comes with grammar, context, and a little story, which gives your memory more to hold on to. Compare learning 춥다 = “to be cold” with learning this:

밖이 너무 추워요.
It’s very cold outside.

From that one sentence you also see that 춥다 changes to 추워요 in everyday speech, that it is used for weather, and that 너무 means “very” or “too”.

Sentences are especially important in Korean because many English words split into two Korean words depending on the situation. Sentences show you the difference:

EnglishKoreanExample
hot (weather)덥다오늘은 정말 더워요.
It’s really hot today.
hot (to the touch)뜨겁다커피가 뜨거워요.
The coffee is hot.
cold (weather)춥다밖이 너무 추워요.
It’s very cold outside.
cold (to the touch)차갑다물이 차가워요.
The water is cold.
to wear (clothes)입다따뜻한 옷을 입으세요.
Wear warm clothes.
to wear (shoes, socks)신다양말을 신으세요.
Put on your socks.

A word list would give you “hot” twice and leave you guessing. Two short sentences settle it.

Tip: keep the sentences short and simple. A sentence you can read in two seconds is better than an impressive one you have to decode.

2. Write new words by hand

Writing by hand is slower than typing, and for vocabulary that slowness is part of the point. To write 냉장고 (refrigerator) you have to notice every letter: that it is 냉 with a final ㅇ, that the middle syllable is 장 and not 잔. When you type, autocomplete and your eyes can skip those details.

Handwriting also helps with Hangul itself. Many beginners can recognize a syllable block but still hesitate over similar-looking letters such as ㅓ and ㅏ, or ㄹ and ㄷ. Writing them a few hundred times in real words removes that hesitation.

To be honest about the limits: handwriting is not magic, and copying a word twenty times without thinking does very little. What helps is attentive writing: say the word aloud as you write it, write it in a sentence, and then try to write it again from memory a minute later. Three mindful repetitions beat twenty mechanical ones.

3. Test yourself instead of rereading

This is the single biggest change most learners can make. Instead of looking at 도서관 = library again, cover the Korean and ask yourself: “How do I say library?” Then check.

Trying to recall a word, even when you fail, is a much stronger workout for memory than reading it. Memory researchers call this retrieval practice, and it is one of the most consistently supported ideas in the study of learning. It also gives you honest feedback: you find out which words you actually know instead of which words look familiar.

Ways to test yourself on paper:

  • Fold the page. Write Korean on the left and English on the right, fold the page down the middle, and quiz yourself in both directions.
  • Fill the gap. Copy an example sentence with the key word left blank: ________에서 책을 빌렸어요. (I borrowed a book from the ___.) Come back the next day and fill it in.
  • Word searches and puzzles. Spotting a word in a grid of Hangul is a light form of recognition practice. It is easier than recall, so treat it as a warm-up or a relaxing review, not your main study.

4. Space out your review

If you review a word five times in one evening, you will remember it tomorrow. If you review it five times spread over two weeks, you are much more likely to remember it next month. This is the spacing effect, first described by the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus in the 1880s and observed many times since. Flashcard apps with “spaced repetition” are built on it.

You do not need an app to use spacing. A simple paper schedule works well:

WhenWhat to do
Day 1Learn the word: read it aloud, write it three times, copy the example sentence.
Day 2Cover and recall. Mark any word you missed.
Day 4Recall again. Write a new sentence with one word swapped.
Day 8Recall again, mixed in with newer words.
Day 15 and laterQuick check. If you still know it, review it again in about a month.

Exact intervals matter much less than the principle: review just before you would forget, and increase the gap each time you succeed. Missed a word? Put it back to the start. That is not failure, it is the system working.

Bonus: group words that share a syllable

A large part of Korean vocabulary comes from Chinese characters, and the same syllable often carries the same meaning across many words. Once you notice this, new words get easier:

  • 학 (learning): 학교 school, 학생 student, 유학 studying abroad
  • 식 (food, eating): 식당 restaurant, 음식 food
  • 관 (building, hall): 도서관 library, 대사관 embassy

Be careful: identical syllables do not always share a meaning, so use this as a memory hook rather than a rule.

A weekly routine that puts it all together

About 20 minutes a day, five days a week:

  1. 5 minutes: review yesterday’s and older words by covering and recalling.
  2. 10 minutes: learn five new words: read aloud, write each three times, copy one example sentence per word.
  3. 5 minutes: write two sentences of your own, each changing one word from an example.

Five new words a day sounds slow. But at five days a week it adds up to about 100 words a month, and with spaced review you will still know most of them at the end of the month. That is how a vocabulary actually grows: small, steady, and tested.
